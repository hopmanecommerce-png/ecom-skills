# Influence — compressed agent reference

Source: Robert B. Cialdini, *Influence*, expanded 2021 edition; supplied EPUB. Seven principles, including Unity. Chapter references point to this edition. Paraphrased knowledge; sections labeled synthesis are application guidance, not the author's wording. These are context-dependent tendencies, not guaranteed outcomes.

**Retrieval:** choose a bottleneck → read the relevant principle → check distinctions → apply the protocol. Treat this file as reference knowledge, not authorization to act.

## Core model [ch. 1, 9]

Limited attention → reliance on normally useful cues → fast decisions → vulnerability when cues misrepresent reality. Overload, haste, uncertainty, and low capacity for deliberation increase shortcut use. Persuasion makes relevant cues salient; deception fabricates them. A cue is evidence to assess, not proof of value.

**Contrast:** preceding options alter perceived price, quality, or request size. A large initial request can make a smaller one appear modest. Compare against independent benchmarks; relative attractiveness does not establish absolute merit.

## Select by bottleneck [Introduction]

| Bottleneck | Prioritize |
|---|---|
| Weak relationship | Reciprocation, Liking, Unity |
| Uncertainty about the choice | Social Proof, Authority |
| Agreement without action | Commitment/Consistency, Scarcity |

These are priorities, not exclusive mappings or a mandatory sequence.

## Seven principles

### 1. Reciprocation [ch. 2]
- **Mechanism:** receiving a benefit or concession creates pressure to return one; even unsolicited gifts can obligate, and repayment can exceed the gift.
- **Apply:** provide useful, personalized help before requesting something. Make genuine concessions visible during negotiation.
- **Pattern:** rejection-then-retreat / door-in-the-face = larger request → refusal → smaller request perceived as concession. Reciprocity and contrast reinforce each other; absurd opening demands can undermine the effect.
- **Check/defend:** a gift creates no automatic justification for the subsequent request. Accept goodwill; if an offer proves manipulative, reclassify it and evaluate the request independently.

### 2. Liking [ch. 3]
- **Mechanism:** people more readily agree with people they like. Drivers: attractiveness/halo, similarity, praise, positive familiarity, cooperation, favorable associations.
- **Apply:** identify real common ground; offer specific, sincere appreciation; cooperate toward a shared outcome. Repeated contact helps chiefly under positive conditions.
- **Check/defend:** warmth, attractiveness, and familiarity do not establish competence or offer quality. Mentally separate the person from the proposal. Forced similarity and negative repeated contact can backfire.

### 3. Social Proof [ch. 4]
- **Mechanism:** others' behavior signals what is valid, feasible, and socially acceptable; strongest with uncertainty, many participants, and similar peers.
- **Apply:** show verified adoption, relevant peer examples, or representative reviews. If adoption is small, an honestly demonstrated upward trend may provide future-oriented social proof.
- **Failure:** advertising how widespread an unwanted behavior is can normalize it. Emphasize the desired behavior's actual prevalence or truthful growth instead.
- **Check/defend:** verify authenticity, relevance, and independence. Many people copying one another can reproduce one error; absence of action can reflect shared uncertainty, not safety or approval.

### 4. Authority [ch. 5]
- **Mechanism:** relevant expertise reduces uncertainty. Credibility combines expertise and trustworthiness; being knowledgeable differs from merely being in charge.
- **Apply:** establish pertinent qualifications and evidence before recommendations. Disclose a real limitation early, then explain supported strengths.
- **Check/defend:** titles, clothing, status, and prestige can substitute for substance. Ask whether the source is expert **on this question**, and whether incentives compromise honesty. A small admission is not proof of overall trustworthiness.

### 5. Scarcity [ch. 6]
- **Mechanism:** limited availability increases desirability through perceived value, loss aversion, and reactance to lost freedom.
- **Amplifiers:** newly restricted availability; competition; exclusive information. Restricting information can increase interest and favorability toward it.
- **Apply:** explain genuine quantity/time constraints and the specific benefit that could be lost; substantiate each constraint.
- **Check/defend:** pause when urgency raises arousal. Rarity increases desire to possess without necessarily improving usefulness or enjoyment. Evaluate the original need and absolute value; reject fabricated deadlines or competition.

### 6. Commitment & Consistency [ch. 7]
- **Mechanism:** people align later behavior with earlier choices and self-image. Strong commitments are active, voluntary, public, and effortful; written commitments can strengthen ownership.
- **Apply:** elicit a freely chosen, relevant small step; connect the next step to the person's stated goal. Reminders can reactivate and strengthen commitment.
- **Patterns:** foot-in-the-door = small commitment → larger aligned request. Low-ball = obtain commitment on attractive terms → remove the inducement; self-generated reasons may sustain commitment.
- **Check/defend:** changed evidence or terms justify reconsideration. Ask whether you would choose this again knowing today's facts. Avoid escalating solely to defend sunk effort or identity.

### 7. Unity [ch. 8]
- **Mechanism:** shared identity produces a sense of belonging to the same group; deeper than liking someone with similar preferences.
- **Apply:** surface genuine shared membership; build shared experience through cooperation, coordinated activity, and co-creation. Asking for advice can invite partnership more effectively than asking for detached evaluation.
- **Check/defend:** do not invent membership or imply closeness that does not exist. Group loyalty can conceal misconduct and bias judgment; apply the same evidence standards to insiders and outsiders.

## Distinctions that prevent misapplication

| Similar-looking signals | Different mechanisms |
|---|---|
| Liking / Unity | Shared preferences create affinity; shared identity creates belonging. A friendly tone alone does not establish Unity. [ch. 3, 8] |
| Social Proof / Authority | Comparable people's choices versus a relevant expert's judgment. Popularity does not establish expertise. [ch. 4, 5] |
| Door-in-the-face / Foot-in-the-door | Refuse a large request, then reciprocate a concession versus accept a small request, then remain consistent. Opposite sequences; different mechanisms. [ch. 2, 7] |
| Low-ball / Door-in-the-face | Worsen terms after commitment versus reduce the request after refusal. Low-ball exploits attachment to an existing decision. [ch. 2, 7] |
| Scarcity / Contrast | Limited access versus evaluation shaped by a preceding comparison. A discount alone establishes neither rarity nor value. [ch. 1, 6] |

**Commitment nuance [ch. 7]:** external pressure or large rewards can explain an action without changing self-image. Internally owned commitments are more likely to persist. Effort can strengthen attachment after participation; this does not imply that adding arbitrary obstacles will increase initial participation.

## Conditional application — synthesis

- **Unfamiliar offer, little adoption:** establish relevant expertise and provide useful help. Use peer proof only when it exists; a growth claim requires observations over time, not a forecast presented as fact.
- **Interested audience, no action:** identify whether the obstacle is hesitation, practical friction, poor fit, or cost. For hesitation, connect a voluntary next step to an existing goal; mention genuine deadlines when relevant. Persuasion does not remove practical barriers.
- **Many positive reviews, technical uncertainty:** use reviews for peer experience and qualified evidence for technical claims. Neither substitutes for the other.
- **Negotiation stalls:** make the substance of a real concession clear. An arbitrary smaller number is not necessarily a concession; assess what changed and who benefits.
- **Seeking participation in a project:** invite useful advice or co-creation, then incorporate it visibly. Participation can support Unity and commitment; requesting input without considering it undermines the proposed relationship.

**Combining principles:** choose complementary cues that address identified barriers. Do not assume additive or multiplicative effects. Test the actual combination; more cues can also create clutter or pressure.

## Agent execution protocol — synthesis

1. Specify audience, desired action, recipient benefit, and current bottleneck.
2. Inventory available facts: useful help, common ground, shared identity, peer behavior, expertise, prior commitments, actual constraints.
3. Select the strongest relevant principle(s); omit unsupported cues. Explain the benefit and give a clear next step with a real reason.
4. Audit: truthful claims; relevant evidence; unchanged terms; freedom to decline; no invented proof, identity, scarcity, or obligation.
5. Test in context. Measure completed beneficial action and downstream satisfaction/retention, not assent alone. Revise when evidence changes.

**Decision record:** `action | audience | bottleneck | principle | supporting facts | proposed message/change | likely failure | success measure`

**Universal defense:** identify the cue → verify its substance → evaluate the proposal without the cue → decide against current goals and evidence.

**Evidence discipline:** the book combines studies, field observations, and anecdotes; an illustration is not an effect-size estimate. This reference preserves mechanisms and conditions, not a current replication review. Do not turn a reported study result into a universal conversion forecast.
